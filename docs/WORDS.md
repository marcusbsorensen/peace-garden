# What forty-three languages said about peace

Every bank in this app was commissioned separately. Each writer was asked, at the
end, for *a word in this language for peace, quiet or meeting that says something
the subthemes do not* — and none of them saw another's answer.

They kept arriving at the same places.

That is what this document is for. Not a glossary: a record of the convergences,
because **several unrelated languages reaching the same insight independently is
a different kind of fact from any one of them saying it**. Turkish and Basque
have no common ancestor. Arabic and Hebrew do; Maltese has both roots and uses
them for different jobs. Slovene kept a grammatical number for exactly two and so
did Arabic.

It is also here because Marcus proposed something with it — see *What this might
become* at the foot.

---

## 1. Peace is something two people do to each other

The round-one finding was Turkish **barış**, built on *barışmak*: to make peace
*with one another*. Peace named as a reciprocal act between two people rather
than as a state or an absence — which is what this app is about, arrived at from
outside the family that named the subtheme.

Eight more languages had it, and several hold it in the grammar rather than in a
word:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Basque** | **elkar**, the reciprocal pronoun — *not a verb but a piece of the grammar*. Basque cannot say two people met without it. Half its social vocabulary hangs off it: *elkartu* (join), *elkarte* (association), *elkarrizketa* (a conversation, literally each-other-speech). |
| **Arabic** | **التعارف**, the reciprocal form of *to know*, whose morphology forbids one participant — لا يتعارف واحد. |
| **Lithuanian** | **susitaikyti**, to reconcile, is reflexive: the grammar refuses to let you do it alone. And **taika**, peace, sits on *taikyti*, to fit one thing to another — not a cessation, a fit. |
| **Croatian** | **pomiriti se**, reflexive-reciprocal. Peace is made *between* two rather than granted to one. |
| **Belarusian** | **памірыцца** cannot take one subject. |
| **Slovak** | **zmieriť sa s niekým** — reconciled *with* somebody; **zmier** is the live legal term for a settlement the court only ratifies, *because the two of them made it*. |
| **Ukrainian** | does it as a productive class: **миритися, вітатися, знайомитися, обніматися** — the *-ся* suffix makes verbs that cannot be conjugated alone. You cannot вітатися by yourself. |
| **Serbian** | **мирбожати се** — a reflexive verb for exchanging *Мир Божји* and a kiss on Christmas morning, everyone with everyone. It exists only reciprocally and only on one day, and Vuk records that in Risan people long at odds are reconciled by it. |

And two languages keep a grammatical number for exactly two, which is the same
idea one level down:

- **Slovene** keeps a full **dvojina**, the dual, which most Slavic languages
  lost. *Ljubiva se* is a sentence three people cannot say.
- **Arabic** keeps **المثنى**. Two people meeting is its own number.

## 2. Peace is wholeness — nothing outstanding, nothing missing

- **Arabic ســلام** sits on س ل م, a root meaning *intact*: it gives سلامة
  (soundness) and سليم (a thing with nothing taken from it). Peace is the
  condition of something that has not been diminished — and the same word is the
  greeting, so you meet a stranger by wishing them wholeness.
- **Hebrew שלום** sits on ש־ל־ם: שלם (whole), השלים (to complete, and *to come to
  terms with*), תשלום (a payment), השלמה (making up what is missing). A debt is
  settled by being *made whole*; a sick person is wished רפואה שלמה, a complete
  healing.

Two agents, two banks, no contact, same joint. And then:

- **Maltese has both roots at once.** *Paċi* is Latin and is what you make at the
  end of a quarrel. *Sliem* is Semitic and is what you say when you meet
  somebody — and *sellem*, to greet, is the same root. **One word ends something,
  the other opens it.**

## 3. Peace is stillness — and a seed is at peace

The most surprising convergence, because it is technical. **Five languages use
their word for peace as the botanical term for seed dormancy.**

- **Slovene** — dormancy is *mirovanje*, from **mir**.
- **Latvian** — a seed sits in its *miera periods*, from **miers**.
- **Ukrainian** — dormancy is **стан спокою**, literally *the state of peace*.
- **Macedonian** — **мирување**, *being at peace*, which is also the physics term
  for a body at rest, where the forces have equalised rather than gone away.
- **Russian** — **покой**, rest; in the plural, *покои*, chambers.

So in five languages `waiting.heldBack` and the whole peace theme are the same
word. A seed waiting under the soil is not inert; it is at peace. This app grows
plants from seeds, and five languages had already said what that means.

Beside them:

- **Japanese 凪 (nagi)** is a character made in Japan: 風 over 止, wind stopped.
  Peace as an observable state of air and water, and it has an hour — 朝凪 and
  夕凪, the slack while land breeze and sea breeze change places.
- **Arabic هدنة**, a truce, is built on ه د ن, *to grow still* — a quiet-word
  doing duty where other languages use a war-word with the fighting taken out.
- **Irish síth / síd** is peace *and* the mound of the otherworld people, both
  from a root meaning *to sit, to settle*. Peace is a place you settle into. And
  the mound's inhabitants are *na daoine sí*, the people of peace — a name given
  out of caution.
- **Icelandic grið** is a peace that is *given*, effective the moment it is
  spoken and running for a stated term, with *griðastaður*, a place where no one
  may lay a hand on you. Its descendant *friðland* was medieval land where a man
  was safe and is now, in Icelandic law, land where the living things are.
- **Albanian besa** is a given word working as a measured quantity of suspended
  time — the Kanun grades it by the clock, twenty-four hours or a month. Peace
  priced in hours.

## 4. Peace is a place, and sometimes it is the world

- **Russian мир** is peace, the world, *and* the village commune — one word for
  all three, and until 1918 the first two were distinguished only on the page:
  *миръ* against *міръ*. Peace as a place containing people.
- **Bulgarian миръ** in Old Church Slavonic is peace and world together — not the
  absence of fighting but the whole settled order of what is there.
- **Croatian mir** descends from the same, *and* has a homophone: in čakavian and
  Dalmatian coastal speech **mir** is a wall, from Latin *mūrus*, and the ruins of
  old drystone walls are **mirine**. On the islands the word for peace and the
  word for a wall are the same sound, and the plural of the wall is what is left
  standing after everyone has gone.
- **Greenlandic eqqissineq** is a person's inner calm *and* a place left
  undisturbed — the root of *nuna eqqissisimatitaq*, the name of the National
  Park: land that has been made to be left at peace. **The largest protected area
  on earth is named with the vocabulary of quiet rather than of law.**

And three languages name shelter by its silence rather than its warmth:

- **Ukrainian затишок**, the corner the wind does not reach, built on *тихо*.
- **Belarusian зацішак**, the same.
- **Greek απάνεμο**, a noun for the place the wind does not reach — not calm as
  weather or as a state of mind, but a spot on the ground you turn a corner into.

## 5. A meeting is a piece of luck

- **Slovene sreča / srečati** — luck and meeting, one root, so *srečanje*, a
  meeting, is built out of good fortune. The claim that a meeting is luck is
  morphology rather than sentiment.
- **Serbian срећа / сусрет** — from *sъrětja*, *that which comes to meet you*.
  Luck is not a quality of a meeting; luck **is** a meeting, named as the thing
  you walked into. The feast Сретење is the same word.
- **Croatian sreća / susret** — the same root again.
- **Bulgarian сряща**, the older form of *среща*, meant not only a meeting but
  *what a meeting settles* — an encounter read as an omen, with *добра сряща*
  wished to somebody setting out.

That last one is uncomfortably close to what this app does. A pair's theme is
rolled at the first crossing and never moves again. **Bulgarian already had a
word for a meeting that decides something permanent.**

## 6. A meeting is a place, a number, or a shove

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Estonian** | **kohtuma**, to meet, is built on *koht*, a place — a coming-into-one-place. The same root gives *kohus*, the court. |
| **Greenlandic** | **ataatsimiinneq**, meeting, is *being in one place*, built on **ataaseq**, one. And **-qatigiipput** ("they do it together") cannot take a singular ending. |
| **Irish** | **aonach**, a fair, from *óen*, one. A gathering named for the becoming-single rather than for the arriving. |
| **Welsh** | **cwrdd** is *to meet*, *a meeting*, and *a chapel service and its congregation* — and it is a clipping of *cyhwrdd*, from *cy-* + *hwrdd*, **a shove**. In Welsh a meeting is a collision, and the collision is also the assembly. |
| **Latvian** | **saticība**, the ordinary word for concord and people living together well, is built directly on **satikt**, to meet, with nothing added. The word for getting along *is* the word for meeting. |
| **Icelandic** | **fundur** is one word for a find and for a meeting: *sá á fund sem finnur*, he owns the find who finds it. |
| **Slovak** | **zíde sa** means both *to gather* and *to come in handy*. A meeting and a usefulness are one sense, not two. |
| **Lithuanian** | **sutikti** is to meet *and* to agree — and specifically the *chance* encounter, since a separate verb covers going out to meet an arrival. Meeting a stranger and coming round to them are the same act. |
| **Hebrew** | **נ־כ־ר** gives both הכיר (to recognise, to know someone) and נכרי (a stranger). The verb for meeting somebody is the verb for un-strangering them. |
| **Greek** | **απαντώ** is to answer somebody and to meet them in the road, one verb — so an answer is a kind of encounter. |
| **Maltese** | **laqgħa**, a meeting, from *laqa'*, to receive somebody in: a meeting is grammatically a door being opened. And **merħba**, welcome, is built on a root meaning *width* — to welcome somebody is to tell them there is room. |
| **Japanese** | **縁 (en)** is the Buddhist term for the conditions under which something comes about, and also the veranda, and the marriage tie. A meeting is not an event but a relation that was already the case and has now become visible. |

Two languages name the *walk toward* a meeting, which no subtheme does:

- **Korean 마중** (going out to meet someone arriving) and **배웅** (going out with
  someone leaving) — both name leaving your own house to stand in the space
  between.
- **Greek καλή αντάμωση** — the Greek goodbye is a meeting. It does not wish you
  well on your way; it names the next time and treats it as settled.

And one names the road by the person on it:

- **Belarusian гасцінец** is a highway *and* the present a visitor brings, both
  from *госць*, a guest. **Ukrainian гостинець** is the same. The road is named
  after the person who arrives on it.

## 7. Greeting

- **Korean 안녕 (安寧).** Korean does not greet by wishing health or a good day —
  it asks whether you are at peace. Both characters mean *tranquil*; the greeting
  is the same word doubled, and it is a question. **Every hello in the language is
  an enquiry after the other person's quiet.**
- **Arabic سلام** and **Maltese sliem** — greeting as handing over wholeness.
- **Japanese 挨拶** — the word for a greeting originally meant a Zen exchange in
  which two practitioners question each other to find where the other stands. Its
  dictionary senses still include *mediation* and *arbitration*.
- **Bulgarian добре дошъл / добре заварил** — a greeting that is one sentence
  split between two people, neither half standing alone. The arriver is told
  *well come*; the arriver answers *well found*.
- **Serbian побратиме** — Vuk records that a Serb who does not know your name
  simply calls you *sworn brother*.

## 8. Quiet, which was the hard subtheme in every language

Every bank struggled with `quietAsASound` and every bank got there. What they
found:

- **Icelandic hljóð** is *a sound* — but *að biðja sér hljóðs* is to ask for
  **silence**, and *hljóður* means silent. One word carries the sound and its
  absence, so quiet is something you are **granted by others** rather than a state
  of the air.
- **Greenlandic -palaaq** builds a verb meaning *it can be heard that something is
  happening*, and the affix is **avalent** — it names no subject at all. There is
  a grammatical slot for a sound with nobody attached to it. Its sibling **-paluk**
  turns any verb into the noun *the sound of Vb-ing*.
- **Japanese 間 (ma)** — in Japanese music the silence is counted as a beat.
  `quietAsASound` says quiet is audible; 間 makes it a unit.
- **Maltese** answers with two letters that are silences: **q**, a glottal stop,
  *ittra li hija toqba* — a letter that is a hole; and **għ**, no longer
  pronounced but still written, holding the place of a sound that has gone.
- **Estonian tasa** is *quietly* and *square with someone* at once, so being quiet
  and being settled up are one idea.
- **Slovak hluchavka**, the dead-nettle, is the *deaf* nettle, named for the sting
  it does not give; *hluchý klas* is an empty ear of grain, standing upright
  precisely because there is nothing in it.
- **Welsh gosteg** is silence *and* a lull in wind or rain — the hush and the
  weather doing the hushing are one noun.
- **Bulgarian шума**, foliage, is named from **шум**, noise. The language named
  the trees after the sound they make and then went quiet about it.
- **Russian глухой** — a place called *deaf*: глухой лес, глухая ночь.
- **Irish** splits it three ways: **tost** is a person not speaking, **ciúnas** is
  a place with no noise in it, **suaimhneas** is quiet inside a person and can be
  had in a loud room.
- **Ukrainian бриніти** — a string you can no longer hear but which is still
  trembling. **гомін** — many voices in which none is distinguishable.

## 9. Kinship, friendship, and being reared alongside

- **Lithuanian bičiulis**, the ordinary word for a close friend, is built on
  **bitė**, a bee. Two people who kept bees together in a forest hollow, shared
  the care and split the honey, were *bičiuliai* — and the relationship was
  heritable. **The word for friend is the word for the person you keep bees with.**
- **Welsh cyfaill**, from Celtic *\*kom-alt-*, "one reared alongside you" —
  friendship as co-fostering rather than blood. And **Cymro**, from *\*kom-brogos*,
  people of the same *bro*: the name of the nation is a statement about sharing
  ground.
- **Albanian mik** is one word for friend, for guest, and in the plural for the
  family you are joined to by marriage. It is Latin *amicus* — and *shtëpi*,
  house, most likely comes from *hospitium*, the guest-lodging. **Both the
  Albanian word for house and the word for friend are Roman words about receiving
  strangers.**
- **Irish scáth** is shadow and shelter in one word, which is why the best-known
  proverb in the language is *Ar scáth a chéile a mhaireann na daoine* — people
  live in each other's shadow, and that is the same as saying they live in each
  other's shelter.
- **Korean 정 (jeong)** is an attachment that *drops on you* — 정이 든다, never
  정을 들인다 — over shared time, which you notice only when it leaves, and which
  attaches to people you dislike. A bond indifferent to whether you wanted it.
- **Basque hats**, breath, is the root of *atseden* (rest), *atsegin* (pleasure),
  *atsekabe* (sorrow) and *hasperen* (a sigh). To rest is to take breath — a
  derivation, not a metaphor.
- **Hebrew לפרגן** (from Yiddish *farginen*) — to be glad at another person's good
  fortune without it costing you anything. Hebrew needed a loanword for this and
  took one.
- **Korean 품앗이** — labour lent and repaid, reckoned in days rather than money.

## 10. Words with no English

- **Galician morriña** is stranger than its reputation. The Real Academia Galega
  lists it among twenty-two synonyms for **orballo**, the fine rain that goes on
  all day. The famous word for homesickness is not primarily a word for a feeling
  at all: it is one of this language's many words for drizzle, lent to a state of
  mind. What it adds is **duration** — it is about something that will not stop.
- **Galician agarimo** is one noun for tenderness and for a windbreak; *agarimar*
  means both to caress and to take somebody in out of the weather.
- **Galician luscofusco** names the threshold between day and night *and* between
  night and day — the same word from either side.
- **Estonian arm** is love, grace and a scar in the same four letters.
- **Estonian videvikku pidama**, "to keep the twilight" — the hour between the
  day's work and the evening's, when no lamp was lit and nothing was done.
- **Latvian rimt** — to subside of its own accord. The wind *rimst*, pain *rimst*,
  and nobody stops them.
- **Latvian mājas**, home, exists only in the plural. You cannot have one home in
  Latvian.
- **Slovak pohoda** is fair weather *and* a person with nothing pressing on them;
  *nepohoda* is foul weather and unease. Being at ease is the weather word, used
  of people with no sense of transfer.
- **Icelandic logn**, dead calm, is from a root meaning *bright* — named for how
  still water looks, not how it sounds.
- **Macedonian печалба**, going away to work, sits on **печал**, sorrow: the cost
  is built into the word, and there is no neutral version. And **крај** is both
  *end* and *region* — *роден крај* is where you are from.
- **Serbian завичај**, from *завићи се*, to grow accustomed: the place you are
  from is named as the place you **got used to**, not the place you were born.
- **Russian уют** (and **приют**, refuge) — the snugness of a place where room was
  found for you. Unlike Danish *hygge* it is built on the root for *sheltering
  somebody*.
- **Slovak priedomie**, the strip of ground in front of a house where people sit —
  neither yard nor street, and with a word of its own. And **medza**, the
  unploughed balk between two fields, the same word as *medzi*, "between".
- **Welsh cynefin** — not "home" and not "habitat" but the patch of open mountain
  a flock keeps to of its own accord, never fenced, never taught, passed from ewe
  to lamb by grazing.
- **Welsh clywed**, to hear, is used across Wales for *smell* and *taste* too: one
  verb for every sense but sight.
- **Japanese 縁側 (engawa)** — the strip at the edge of a house where somebody who
  is not a guest can sit and talk without either of you being inside or outside.

## 11. Writing systems that say something

- **Korean.** Hangul's consonants depict the shape the mouth makes: ㄱ the back of
  the tongue at the soft palate, ㅁ the closed lips, ㅅ the tooth. Its vowels are
  built from three strokes standing for heaven, earth and a person. The 1446
  document announcing it says its purpose was that ordinary people who could not
  use Chinese characters should be able to write what they meant.
- **Chinese 閒.** The *Shuowen* gives it as a crack — a door with the moon in it,
  moonlight through the gap between the leaves. That graph splits into 间, an
  interval and also a room, and 闲, leisure and ease. **Being at ease in Chinese is
  not a state a person is in; it is a gap, and specifically the gap that light
  gets through.**
- **Chinese 氣**, glossed as *fodder and grain given to a guest* — the character
  now carrying breath and vital force began as an act of hospitality.
- **Chinese 龢**, the older form of 和, is built on the pan-pipes: harmony, and so
  peace, was a fact about tuned sound before it was ever a fact about people.
- **Greek ειρήνη** is a woman's name, common enough that most readers know an
  Ειρήνη. She was a goddess before she was an abstraction — one of the three Ὧραι
  alongside Εὐνομία and Δίκη, so peace was originally a **season**, something that
  comes round rather than something you achieve.
- **Basque bake**, peace, is a Latin loan — like *eliza* and *errege*. For a
  language with no relatives anywhere, the word for peace came from outside. That
  is a fact about being surrounded rather than descended from.

## What this might become

**Settled 3 September**, and the design is in `docs/WEBSITE.md` §*The ambassador
plants*. It lives in the shared garden only — never in the app, never touching a
person's own seed — which removes the objection below about glossing: the subject
is a fact about another language rather than a passage addressed to somebody.
What follows is the earlier working-out, kept because the third point still holds.

Marcus's idea, 3 September, and it is a good one:

> a sub-section of each theme where etymologies from languages other than the
> speaker's native one are shared — this also brings more understanding of
> others and thereby hopefully more peace.

**It cuts against the app's strongest rule, on purpose, and that is what makes it
interesting.** Everything else here is built so that a passage arrives in the
reader's own language and never announces where anybody else is from: the
passage is drawn from the reader's bank, the sharer's language is never
published, and `docs/WEBSITE.md` spends a section on why partitioning the garden
by language would be the first fact this app stated about somebody they never
chose to state.

This would be the one place the app deliberately shows you a language that is not
yours. Three things it has to answer:

1. **It needs a gloss, and this app has never translated anything.** *Bičiulis is
   built on the word for a bee* is only meaningful in a language the reader has.
   That is a translation — of a *fact about* a language rather than of a passage,
   which may be the distinction that makes it acceptable. Worth being clear that
   it is a real exception rather than pretending it is not one.
2. **Whose language does it show?** Not the sharer's — that publishes them. Drawn
   from the whole set, by the same derivation everything else uses, is the answer
   that stays consistent: the pair's seed picks the language the way it picks the
   passage.
3. **It is a new commission, and the multiplier is now forty-three.** These
   findings exist in English because that is the language the reports came back
   in. Shown to a Finnish reader they would need to be in Finnish.

The cheapest first version answers all three: **one line under a passage, in the
reader's own language, about a word in another one, chosen by the pair's own
seed.** No new page, no new navigation, and it is the same shape as the passage
itself — derived, not stored, and different for each of the two people.

It is worth saying what the whole document is evidence for. Forty-three writers
were asked the same question in isolation and a dozen of them independently found
that peace is a thing two people do *to each other*, that a seed under the soil is
*at peace*, and that a meeting is a piece of *luck*. Whatever this app ends up
showing, that is the finding.
