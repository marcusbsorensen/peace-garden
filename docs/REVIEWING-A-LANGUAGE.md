# Reading Peace Garden in your language

Thank you for doing this. It is about fifteen minutes, and you do not need to
know anything about the app.

Peace Garden is a phone app in which a plant grows from two people meeting. This
is its website — the page somebody lands on when a friend sends them a seed. It
has been translated into forty-two languages by machine, and machine translation
is fluent long before it is right. **You are the part that cannot be automated.**

## What you are actually judging

Not the grammar. Three things, in this order:

1. **Is it true?** Every sentence makes a claim about how the app works. A
   translation can be perfectly natural and say something the app does not do.
   §3 below says what each sentence has to mean.
2. **Does it sound like a person?** Quiet and plain, the way you would explain
   something to one person. Not an advertisement. Nothing here is being sold and
   nobody is being told to do anything.
3. **Would you send it to a friend?** If a sentence would embarrass you, it is
   wrong even if every word is correct.

Say *"this is fine"* where it is fine. It is genuinely useful to know which
parts do not need arguing about.

## 1. Getting in

You will have been sent six links. Open them on a phone if you can — that is
where almost everybody will read this — and otherwise a laptop is fine.

If a page comes up in English, that is worth telling us: it means your language
is missing that sentence and is falling back.

## 2. The six screens

1. **What this is** — for somebody who has no seed yet. Four paragraphs: the
   line under the mark, and three about the app.
2. **A seed that has arrived** — the page a friend's link opens. A plant name,
   who sent it, when it was planted, a quotation, and two paragraphs about
   growing it.
3. **The same, from somebody who gave no name** — one phrase changes, to
   whatever your language would call an unnamed gardener.
4. **A seed that has come back** — the heading changes and nothing else.
5. **A link that arrived broken** — one sentence saying so. It should sound
   matter-of-fact rather than alarming.
6. **The garden** — a map. Mostly names that stay in English on purpose; see §4.

## 3. What each sentence has to mean

**The line under the mark.** A plant comes out of a meeting between two people.
It is a name for the thing rather than a sentence about it — closer to the
subtitle of a book than to a slogan.

**The first paragraph.** The app makes a plant out of two people meeting; two
phones hand each other a seed; what grows carries features of *both*; and it
opens over real days, at its own pace. That last part matters — a reader who
expects a finished picture will think a seedling is a failure. It also uses two
comparisons, a handshake and a shared garden. **If a handshake means something
different where you are, say so** and tell us the nearest ordinary thing two
people do on meeting.

**The second paragraph.** A seed can travel in a link as well as by two phones
touching, and that is how it reaches a phone that has never had the app. It must
not suggest the person has to sign up or install anything first.

**The third paragraph.** A seed reaches you from a person you meet face to face,
and the app exists on iPhone and iPad. **It must not suggest you can get a seed
from the website.** You cannot. That is the whole point of the sentence.

**Growing it** — the important one. It must say: the app crosses this seed with
one of your own; the plant is yours to keep; **this meeting grows one plant, and
both of you have that one**; and it stays the same plant.

It must **not** say, or imply, that the same two seeds always make the same
plant. They do not — meeting the same person again grows a different plant, on
purpose. The English said the wrong thing here for weeks before anybody noticed,
because the false sentence is the one that sounds natural. If your language has
drifted back into it, that is the single most valuable thing you can find.

**The last line.** The app is on iPhone and iPad, and the link keeps, so there
is no hurry. No deadline, no urgency.

## 4. Things that are meant to look like that

Please do not report these — they are decisions, not mistakes.

- **The plant's name is Latin** and is not translated. It is invented, and it is
  the same in every language on purpose.
- **The garden's areas are English** — *The Cold Frame*, *The Seedbed*, *The
  Crossing*. They are proper nouns, like the plant's Latin name.
- **"Peace Garden" is a name** and stays as it is.
- **The quotation is not a translation** of the English one. Each language has
  its own collection, drawn from its own writers. If a quotation is wrong or
  wrongly attributed, that *is* worth reporting, and it is a different job from
  this one.

## 5. Sending it back

Whatever is easiest — a message, a mail, a screenshot with a scribble on it. For
anything you would change, the useful shape is:

> **Screen 2, the "growing it" paragraph.** It says X, which reads as Y. I would
> say Z.

If you only have five minutes rather than fifteen, do screen 1 and the "growing
it" paragraph on screen 2. That is four of the six sentences and the one that
has already been wrong once.
