# The privacy round, 26 September 2026

The eight `privacy*` keys, written in forty-one languages in one pass. They had
been `null` in every catalogue since the English was corrected on 24 September;
a `null` draws the English silently and correctly marked, so until today every
non-English reader of `/privacy` read it in English.

Kalaallisut is out, as it is out of the commission of 24 September, by its own
`awaiting` note: the machine pass was not good enough to ship and nobody on
this side can judge it. That reasoning binds hardest here, where a wrong
sentence is a false claim rather than an awkward one.

Written from `commission.py --privacy <code>`, one session per language, as
every earlier round was. Every check passes: `check.py` 42 clean and 41 with
the privacy page, `app_check.py`, `export.py --check` in step, and the
commission of 24 September's own `sheets.py --check` still 41 of 41.

**Nobody who speaks any of the forty-one has read a word of it.**

## What was verified, and how

The failure this round could have had is a fluent paragraph that quietly drops
a claim, so the checks were on the claims rather than the prose:

- **Only the eight keys moved.** Every catalogue diffed against `HEAD` key by
  key: no collateral edit, no key reordered, no top-level key touched.
- **`privacy2` and `privacy6` hold their lists.** Both were read in full in
  several languages, and every catalogue's length was taken against the
  English: the only strings under 0.75× are Chinese, Japanese and Korean, at
  0.36–0.6×, which is ordinary CJK density. No Latin or Cyrillic language came
  up short, which is what a dropped item would have looked like.
- **Arabic and Hebrew were read in the browser**, not assumed. `#` sits on the
  correct side in both with no marks: it has Arabic or Hebrew on both sides and
  resolves to the paragraph direction on its own, so the Batch 3 precedent of a
  U+200E before a Latin run does not apply and would have pushed it the wrong
  way.

## Three things the round found in the English

These are not translation questions. Forty-one languages hit them from
different directions, which is what makes them worth recording.

1. **The English says *thirty days*; all forty-one say *30*.** The standing
   rule makes a reported quantity a numeral from 2 up, and a retention period
   is the most table-able number on the page. Every language reached the same
   answer independently, Arabic in Eastern Arabic numerals. **The English is
   the outlier, and `check.py` does not check the English.**
2. **"when it was drawn" did not survive, so the English now says *made*.**
   At least eleven languages — the Slavic six, and Welsh, Irish, Greek,
   Turkish and Maltese — reported *when it came about* or *when it appeared*,
   because their lot-drawing verbs read as a lottery or as taking from
   someone. The timestamp was carried everywhere; the claim that the seed is a
   *draw* was not. **Settled the same day: it did not need to be.** On a
   privacy page the item is a timestamp, and that the seed is drawn at random
   is a mechanism described where mechanisms are. `strings.js`, the `PRIVACY`
   claims in `commission.py` and `SettingsView`'s own English all say *when
   that seed was made* now, which is what every language had already found for
   itself. The seed screen's `Drawn` row label is untouched: a different
   string, unambiguous beside a date, and translated in seven.
3. **`check.py`'s seed-term rule was forcing unnatural sentences.** It matched
   `term.lower()[:4]` as a substring, and inflecting languages break the stem
   before the fourth character. Arabic, Italian, Romanian, Macedonian, Welsh
   and Maltese all bent `privacy3` to a singular or a periphrasis to satisfy
   it — «كلّ بذرة وكلّ نبتة عندك», *Ogni seme, ogni pianta*, *orice sămânță a
   ta*, *Твоето семе*, *pob hedyn a phob planhigyn*. Every one is correct and
   none is the first phrasing a writer would reach for.

   **Settled the same day.** The forms of the word are written by hand per
   language in `BY_HAND` in `tools/strings/terms.py`, carried in `terms.json`
   beside the word, and any one of them satisfies the rule; a language with no
   entry still falls back to the head. So the check no longer decides the
   grammatical number, and the six `privacy3` strings were rewritten in the
   plural the English has — with `privacy6` in Arabic, where «بذرة تلك النبتة»
   became «بذرتها». Irish went in at the same time and for the same reason:
   lenition puts an `h` after the first letter, so *do shíolta* has no `síol`
   in it.

## Two decisions taken inside the round

- **Russian and Ukrainian took the shorter word for the heading** —
  *Приватность* / *Приватність* over *Конфиденциальность* /
  *Конфіденційність*. This was measured, not preferred: the heading style is
  uppercase and tracked out, a single word offers no wrap opportunity, and the
  long form runs 293 px against a 264 px column at 320 px. If the legalistic
  word is wanted, the heading style has to give.
- **`privacy7` quotes the app's switch** in the app's seven languages, in each
  language's own quotation marks, because the switch is a full imperative
  clause in all seven and not a noun like English *alerts*. It is the only
  quotation mark in those catalogues.

## The machine cross-check, 26 September

Before any native speaker is asked to read this, all forty-one were put back
through machine translation into English and compared with the source, so that
a reader is handed the two or three places worth their attention rather than
eight paragraphs.

**Method.** Every one of the seven paragraphs, in all forty-one languages, was
back-translated by **Google Translate** — 287 passes — and scored against the
English on content-word overlap. The load-bearing two, `privacy2` and
`privacy6`, were additionally checked claim by claim: a probe for each item
the sheet says the string *must say*, and a probe for each thing it *must
not*. Anything that looked like a real loss was put through a second engine,
**MyMemory**, which is a different system on different data, and anything the
two disagreed about was read by hand against the source.

**A back-translation convicts nobody.** It flags candidates. Half of what it
flagged turned out to be the metric's fault, and that is recorded below too,
because the next person to run this should not re-investigate them.

### What it found, in order of how much it matters

1. **No claim is lost, in any language.** Every item `privacy2` lists as
   crossing between two phones is present in all forty-one, and so is every
   item of `privacy6`.
2. **No language says the thing `privacy6` must never say.** Not one of the
   forty-one back-translates as *nothing is kept* or *deleted everywhere*
   after a plant is taken back. That is the hardest negative on the page and
   the one a regulator would read first. The empty place, the scrambled record
   and the 30 days survive everywhere — as *a messed up record* (pt), *a
   scattered log* (ar), *a mixed mark* (ro), all of them right.
3. **Welsh `privacy2` is the one place a claim may genuinely not land.** The
   fifth item, *whether a place may be kept with the meeting*, is written
   **`a gaiff lle ei gadw gyda'r cyfarfod`**. Welsh `a` opens an indirect
   question and also a relative clause, and **both engines read it as the
   relative**: Google gives *the name you chose to display, which will be kept
   with the meeting*, MyMemory *which place will be reserved with the
   meeting*. Neither sees *whether*. Two independent systems failing the same
   way is the strongest signal this exercise produced. **A Welsh reader should
   look at this sentence before anything else on the page** — and the question
   for them is only whether a fluent reader hears the indirect question where
   a machine does not.
4. **Danish and Slovene fuse two list items in `privacy2`** — *the name you
   chose to show* swallows *whether a place may be kept*, giving *the name
   you've chosen to show whether to save somewhere with the meeting*. **This
   is the English's fault, not the translators'.** Google's own translation of
   the English paragraph into Danish and into Slovene, back again, fuses in
   exactly the same place. Those two grammars cannot keep the comma list apart
   where English barely can. Worth a reader's eye; worth considering whether
   the English sentence should be shorter.

### Flagged and cleared — do not re-investigate

- **German and Romanian** looked like case 4, and are not: Google's own
  translations of the English keep the items apart where ours fuse. Read by
  hand, both are fine — German's relative clause is closed by its verb-final
  `hast` before the comma, which is a distinction English cannot represent, so
  the fusion is an artefact of coming back into English.
- **Japanese 種.** Google back-translates `privacy6` as *the species of the
  plant, the two parent species*; MyMemory reads the same character as *the
  seed of the plant, the two parent seeds*. 種 is simply both, and it is the
  settled term. Not wrong — but it is the one word in Japanese where a reader
  should confirm that the seed sense is the one that lands.
- **`privacy7` scores lowest of the seven in twenty of the forty-one, and that
  is structural.** The English says *alerts* where every language says
  *notifications*, and the app's seven quote the switch's full name — French
  carries the whole of *Notify me when a common seed is shared* where the
  English has one word. The claim is intact everywhere.
- **Chinese, Japanese and Korean score lowest overall** (0.58–0.64 against a
  0.74 median). That is what back-translation from CJK does to function words;
  the claim probes pass.
- **Albanian** renders *a place* with *vend*, which one engine brings back as
  *seat*. Both senses are the word's; a reader can settle it in a moment.

### What this cannot tell you

Machine back-translation tests whether the *claims* survive. It says nothing
about register — whether the page sounds like the rest of the site, whether
the address is right, whether a word is the one a gardener would use. Every
line in the *For the reader* table above is still worth its reader's time.
What this removes is the need to read all eight paragraphs hunting for a
dropped clause: there is not one.

## Left open

- **The app's own privacy paragraph is untranslated in all seven app
  languages.** `SettingsView`'s current English has no localisation anywhere;
  what ships is a superseded shorter string that names one random number and
  adds a claim `privacy2` must not make. So `privacy2` agrees with the shipped
  app text on vocabulary only. **When that string is translated it has to be
  matched to `privacy2`, not to the old one.** The `Joint seeds` section
  heading is missing too.
- **`privacy7`'s alerts switch is named freely in the thirty-four non-app
  languages.** If any of them gains the app, its switch must take the word
  chosen here or the two disagree.
